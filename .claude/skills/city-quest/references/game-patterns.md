# Game patterns for a young child

Challenge, curse and power-up mechanics from travel game shows and party
games (claiming territory, races, tag, hide and seek), distilled into
patterns a family quest can borrow. Use it when designing challenges
(step 5) and when a reviewer asks for a rewrite.

Every pattern keeps the quest's rules: co-op team against the city, duels as
side quests, 2–10 minutes, checkable on the spot, needs being there, and
safe (the child is never alone or out of sight, talks only to shop, café and
museum staff, keeps away from water and rail edges). Respect the profile's
dislikes (e.g. skip the drawing variants for a kid who dislikes drawing).

## Patterns

Each pattern: **why it works** → **for the kid**, with ideas.

**A. Taste the place.** The challenge *is* the travelogue: eating the local
dish teaches you the place. → The city's signature snack, ordered by
the kid at the counter in the local language; a rating; ⚔ a blind taste
test of two or three local sweets or cheeses ("which one is the stroopwafel?").

**B. Perform from memory.** Public, a bit embarrassing, and you watch them
improve over retries. → Learn a short local rhyme or song in the morning and
perform it at a landmark (on one foot for a boss); hum a tune on café
glasses; say a tongue-twister in the local language to a parent.

**C. Look properly.** Forces the players to *see* an ordinary street. → A
parent names 8 things visible from here, the kid finds them all against a
timer; follow one bird, boat or tram with your eyes for 30 seconds without
losing it; ⚔ "I see it first" on a list of local details.

**D. Find and count against a list.** A clear list plus a clock shows
progress. → Find 5 lions / coats of arms / gable stones in one street; a
shop sign for each letter of a word; count the bells, arches or boats — always
a thing *this* place has.

**E. Absurd but concrete feats.** The target is silly and exact; failing is
funny. → Heel-to-toe through a gate passage; hop the length of a building;
walk a square's paving line without stepping off; ⚔ toss a pebble closest to a
paving-stone line. Dry, wide places only.

**F. Build from what's around.** Improvising under a timer with found
materials. → Build the landmark from pebbles, twigs, bark (or a LEGO shop's
demo table) in 5 minutes and photograph it next to the real one.

**G. Bet, then reveal.** The guess is placed out loud, then the reveal gives
tension and a clean verdict. → ⚔ Guess steps to the tower, floors, boats in
the harbour, whistles of the train — then count on the spot; closest wins.
Optional: the kid bets 1–3 coins on succeeding *before* a hard task (double
back on success). Use guess-closest at most once or twice per trip.

**H. Chance.** Anyone can win; swings make drama. → A die picks which of
three streets to take, the size of a reward, or the curse; heads or tails
decides a small detour. Keep luck light: it never takes away progress.

**I. Hold it.** Endurance creates suspense. → Freeze like a statue for 20–60
seconds next to a statue; balance while counting; stay silent until the next
bridge. Short, with a visible end (no "spin until you fall").

**J. Steal, veto, battle.** Risk you choose and swings in the lead. → A duel
winner "steals" one sticker or coin from the other side (cap at one item); a
veto token lets the team skip one task for a small price (1 coin or 10 hops)
— it prevents meltdowns.

**K. Curses with a visible end.** The opponent imposes an absurd handicap:
comedy and reversal. A good curse costs something to cast and lasts a set
time, until a task is done, or until the next question; only one blocking
curse at a time. → Exactly 6 for a die (see challenge-design §2), team-wide or
parent-only, city-themed: right turns only until the next stop (where safe),
talk like a sailor, carry the teddy, walk like a gull, every bridge needs a
password, "build a pebble tower as tall as the kid's" before going on.

**L. Hide-and-seek questions → trails.** Seeker question families
turn into riddles and navigation: *matching* ("is the nearest bakery the one
we can see?"), *measuring* ("are we closer to the church or the station?"),
*radar* ("is it within 100 steps?"), *thermometer* (warmer/colder after each
corner), *photo* (a parent shows a close-up photo, the kid finds the spot),
*tentacles* ("which of these three bridges is it nearest?"). The kid can be
the seeker of a hidden *object* or place — never the hider.

**M. Power-ups.** Spending earned currency on an edge. → Perks bought with
coins: a hint, skip one task, a parent carries the backpack, the kid picks
lunch, the kid casts a curse on a parent.

**N. A judge.** A vote settles a duel. → The other parent, a grandparent on a
video call, or friendly café staff decide which build or pose wins.

**O. The place's own challenge.** The best travel challenges only make sense
in *that* country or town. → The strongest tasks only
make sense here: the unicorn on *this* weigh house, the boys who modelled
*this* statue, the gate that is curved on purpose. Prefer a city-specific
twist on any pattern above over a generic task done at a landmark.

## What does not transfer

- Alcohol, real-money wagers, losing all coins on a capture or steal.
- Being alone, hiding out of sight, leaving a phone in public, GPS chases.
- Getting strangers to perform; contact beyond staff behind a counter.
- Long waits and endurance (15–30 min); 4-hour timers.
- Map maths (km radii, borders, Street View): use steps, landmarks, pictures.
- Stunts at water, bridge or rail edges: make a dry version or drop it.
- Hidden card economies, curse stacking, curses without an end.
